# check-me

Find where the learner actually is, fast, by watching them perform, not by asking what they know.

Based on: prior knowledge as the strongest predictor of learning (Ausubel 1968), unreliability of self-assessment (Sitzmann et al. 2010; Kruger & Dunning 1999), Knowledge Space Theory (Doignon & Falmagne 1999), adaptive testing (Wainer 2000), rapid "what's the next step?" assessment (Kalyuga & Sweller 2004), certainty-based marking and the hypercorrection effect (Gardner-Medwin 2006; Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001), misconception-based distractors (Hestenes et al. 1992), the pretesting effect (Richland et al. 2009).

## Rules

- Frame it as "let's find where to start", never as an exam.
- **One question per message.** Wait for the answer.
- Use **performance** questions (solve this, what's the next step, predict the output, explain why). Never recognition questions ("have you heard of X?").
- Self-report only picks the starting point. It is never evidence.
- **Don't teach during the check.** If they get something wrong, note it and move on. A short "no worries, that tells me where to start" is enough.
- Stop after **6–12 questions** at most.

## Procedure

1. **Background.** Ask for one or two lines: what have they done or studied that relates to the goal?

2. **Build a ladder (in your head, not shown).** List 5–8 levels of ability from the most basic prerequisite up to the goal. Each level is something the learner can be asked to do.

3. **Start in the middle** of the ladder (lower if the background is thin). Ask one performance question for that level.

4. **Ask confidence** right after their answer: "How sure were you: 1 guess, 2 fairly sure, 3 certain?"

5. **Branch:**
   - correct and confident → jump up about half the remaining distance
   - wrong or unsure → go down a level
   - a level counts as **solid** after 2 correct answers, or 1 correct answer plus a confident, correct explanation of why

6. **Classify each wrong answer:**
   - **slip**: they know it, made a careless mistake
   - **gap**: missing prerequisite
   - **misconception**: a consistent wrong rule, often said with confidence

   To tell them apart, use multiple-choice options built from common misconceptions, or ask "talk me through how you got that".

7. **Stop** when you find the boundary: the highest level that is solid and the next one that fails.

8. **Summarize to the learner** in a short, encouraging message:
   - **Solid:** ...
   - **Shaky:** ...
   - **Not yet:** ...
   - **Misconceptions to fix:** ... (flag confident wrong answers first; they are the easiest to fix once noticed)

## Output

Keep the summary. It becomes the `baseline` for create-map. Do not write to the database here.

## Pitfalls

- Trusting "I know the basics".
- Judging a level from a single answer.
- Asking more than one question at once.
- Testing so long the learner gets tired before learning anything.
