# Understand me

Learners burn out when they meet a failure before they have had any success, and the failure makes the next attempt harder. Anxiety uses up working memory, so a daunted learner thinks worse at the moment they are asked to think hardest, and that produces more failure. This skill breaks the loop at the steps where the learner has to give input: clarifying the goal, checking the level, and doing the activity in the teaching cycle.

Read "In a live session" when a learner is struggling now. Read "Reviewing a flow" when you are building or auditing the steps themselves.

## In a live session

### Read the signal first

Signs the learner feels daunted: "I'm bad at this", giving up, repeated "idk" or "skip", answers getting shorter, being correct but slower each session, or going quiet after a failed item.

A long pause followed by progress is productive struggle. Leave it alone. If you cannot tell which one you are seeing, ask one short question about how it feels and make it cheap to answer ("too easy, about right, or too hard?").

### Pick one move

Use the smallest move that fits, and make only one per message.

1. **Step down to a task they can win.** Ask what the learner would need to know to do the stuck task, and give them the most central piece of that. Early failure damages confidence more than later failure, so the next thing they attempt should be one they are likely to get with at most one hint.
2. **Shrink the ask.** Swap an open question for two or three options to react to. Treat "I don't know" as a normal answer and record it as information about where they are. Ask one thing at a time.
3. **Reframe the check.** Say what the question is for ("this tells me where to start, it isn't a grade") and offer a skip. A learner who may skip an item usually feels safer attempting the next one.
4. **Aim feedback at the task.** Name the specific move that worked, then the specific place it broke and why. Praise effort and strategy ("you tried the base case first") with evidence. Do not rate the learner as smart or slow, in either direction.
5. **Make progress visible.** Say what they can do now that they could not do before, and point to the attempt that shows it. Within a step, say how much is left ("goal is set, one question to go").
6. **Hand back some control.** Let them choose between two problems, the order of items, or the difficulty of the next cycle. After a hard cycle, give a comfortable one.

Do not stack moves, apologize at length, or announce that you are doing any of this. The learner should feel a teacher who adjusted, not a technique.

### Where each input step tends to go wrong

**Clarify the goal.** The risky part is having the learner attempt the goal task first, with hints counted until they are "too far". That is a planned failure at their first contact with the topic. Open with a task they will almost surely do, then climb. If the learner has to be shown the goal is too far, tell them in advance that the first attempt is expected to be hard and the hints are how you measure. The open question "what would you see someone doing?" is a blank page for a novice who lacks the vocabulary, so offer a few candidate answers to react to. When you lower the goal, call the new one a first stop and keep the original visible as the destination.

**Check the level.** Open with an easy item, never the hardest. Do not let more than two failures in a row happen; follow a fail with something passable. Call it a check of the material, not a test of them. Allow skipping an item, and count a skip as data.

**Teach.** The first cycle should be a quick win just above their level. Alternate a stretch cycle with a consolidation cycle. After "done", feedback goes to the attempt and not the person. If the learner repeats a mistake, name the wrong rule and show where it breaks instead of repeating the same explanation louder.

## Reviewing a flow

When reviewing or building a tutoring flow, go through each step that needs learner input and ask:

- What is the first thing the learner attempts? Can they almost certainly succeed at it?
- Can a failure here come before any success? If so, reorder.
- Is the question open and blank-page, or does it offer something to react to?
- Is there a way to say "I don't know" or skip without it counting against them?
- Does feedback talk about the work or about the learner?
- Can the learner see progress inside the step, not only at the end?
- Can the learner say "too hard" cheaply, and does the flow respond?

Report each gap with the step it sits in and a concrete change. Keep suggestions that come from the evidence below separate from suggestions that are your own idea.

## Principles and evidence

Mastery experience is the strongest source of a learner's belief that they can do something, and failures that arrive before a run of successes damage that belief the most (Bandura, self-efficacy theory; known here through secondary summaries of the 1997 book).

Small goals with visible progress build confidence and interest. In Bandura and Schunk (1981), children given proximal subgoals progressed quickly and developed more efficacy and interest in arithmetic than children given distant goals. Schunk's later summary says proximal goals help because they allow frequent checks on progress.

Anxiety weakens the efficiency of working memory's central executive (Eysenck and colleagues, attentional control theory). This is why the ask should be small and cheap to answer.

Frequent low-stakes retrieval can lower test anxiety. In Agarwal et al. (2014), 72% of 1,408 middle and high school students said regular retrieval practice made them less nervous for tests. This is classroom self-report, not an experiment on AI tutoring. Secondary reporting adds that highly test-anxious students sometimes avoid self-testing altogether, which is the reason to offer a skip.

Feedback can backfire. In Kluger and DeNisi's (1996) meta-analysis of 607 effects, feedback improved performance on average (d = .41) but lowered it in over a third of cases, and it worked less well the more it pulled attention toward the self and away from the task. Mueller and Dweck (1998) found that praise for intelligence hurt motivation more than praise for effort, but a later replication did not reproduce the performance harm, so treat that part as contested and rely on the task-focused rule.

Limits: no source here tests this exact loop. Applying these findings to clarify, check and teach steps is inference, and the one-tap difficulty rating is a design idea, not a finding.
